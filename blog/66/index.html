They All Thought It Was Forever

June 22, 2026 / Also on Substack

They all thought it was real.

Not just real in the ordinary sense, not real like weather or hunger or the ache in your bones after a day of work. Real in the deeper and more dangerous sense: permanent. Obvious. The stage on which life would continue indefinitely.

The speakeasies. The flappers. The beatniks. The leather jackets. The hippies in rose-colored glasses, looking out at the world as if the future were simply a longer version of the present with better music.

The Sumerians with their clay tablets.

The Egyptians with their instruments and incense and impossible geometry.

The Romans with their law, their roads, their military logic, their marble confidence.

Every age looks at itself and mistakes the costume for the body.

Every civilization stands inside its own weather and calls it the sky

The Illusion of Continuity

Human beings are not built to understand historical acceleration.

We are built to wake up in a world that resembles yesterday. The trees are still there. The neighbors still exist. The language still works. The sun comes up with the same old indifference. The rituals continue. The market opens. The school bus arrives. The coffee tastes like coffee.

This creates the illusion that the world is basically stable.

It is not.

The world is a moving platform. Culture is not a cathedral. It is foam on a wave. Your assumptions are not bedrock. They are scaffolding, and most of it was assembled shortly before you arrived.

People imagine that history is slow because the individual life is short. But this is a trick of scale. From the point of view of a person, institutions appear massive. From the point of view of time, they are mayflies.

A fashion. A nation. A profession. A class structure. A moral consensus. A musical movement. A medium. A form of childhood. A way of dating. A way of dying.

Born, metabolized, discarded.

We say civilization as if it were a noun.

It is closer to weather.

The Hyperorganisms Die Too

A society is not a machine, exactly. Machines are too simple. Societies are closer to living systems: hungry, adaptive, territorial, confused, brilliant, self-deceiving.

A city has a metabolism.

An empire has a nervous system.

A religion, a market, a bureaucracy, a school system, a fashion scene, a military, a social network, a profession—each behaves like a strange organism made of human attention. It recruits bodies. It captures time. It reproduces by copying patterns from mind to mind. It defends itself. It mutates.

And then, eventually, it dies.

Not always dramatically. Not always with fire at the gates. Sometimes the organism dies while the buildings remain. The rituals continue for a while after the meaning has left them, like electrical impulses twitching through a corpse.

The clay tablet becomes a museum object.

The temple becomes a ruin.

The uniform becomes a costume.

The slang becomes a joke.

The future keeps eating the furniture.

This is the part we resist. We can accept that individual people die. We can accept that animals die, that forests burn, that stars exhaust their fuel. But we struggle to accept that entire modes of life die too.

Not just civilizations. Smaller things. Intimate things.

The shape of an afternoon.

The sound of a teenager’s bedroom.

The way people used to wait.

The way people used to get lost.

The way boredom used to feel before every pocket contained a portal.

These are not minor details. These are the texture of being alive.

And they vanish.

Your Life Is Not Normal

Nothing about your life is typical.

Almost no humans in history have experienced anything like the world you have lived in. Not the light switches. Not the antibiotics. Not the supermarkets. Not the airplanes. Not the screens. Not the global nervous system vibrating in your hand.

For most of human existence, night was actually dark.

Distance was real.

Food was uncertain.

Music disappeared after it was played.

A face could be lost forever.

A question might remain unanswered for a lifetime.

Now we live in a hallucination of access. We can summon voices from the dead, look at storms from orbit, speak across oceans, manufacture synthetic dreams, and watch strangers perform personality for invisible crowds.

This feels normal only because you were placed inside it.

A fish does not experience water as an invention.

But this world is an invention. More precisely, it is a temporary configuration of energy, infrastructure, incentives, minerals, software, logistics, law, empire, and myth.

It is not the human condition.

It is a recent operating system.

And it will be replaced.

The Future Isn’t What It Used To Be

Yogi Berra supposedly said, “The future ain’t what it used to be.”

This is funny because it is metaphysically correct.

Every generation inherits not only a present, but a future. A model of where things are going. Children grow up inside a corridor of expectation. The future has a smell. A color palette. A soundtrack. A silhouette.

For one generation, the future was chrome, rockets, monorails, glass domes, and atomic kitchens.

For another, it was revolution, communes, free love, and psychic liberation.

For another, it was cyberspace, neon grids, hacker gods, and the melting of meat into information.

For another, it was optimization, startups, platforms, personal brands, and the quantification of everything.

But the imagined future almost never arrives.

Not because nothing happens.

Because something else happens.

The future you imagined as a child did not fail to arrive because history stopped. It failed because a different future was already unfolding underneath your feet while you were still making plans with the old map.

That is the wound of retrofuturism.

It is not merely nostalgia for old predictions. It is grief for timelines that never condensed into reality. The jetpacks, the lunar hotels, the cheerful robots, the cities that looked like clean appliances floating above the Earth.

But every retrofuture is also a confession: people were always wrong about what was permanent.

They mistook the visible curve for the deeper trajectory.

They thought the surface aesthetics of their age would continue forward, polished and upgraded. They did not understand that the substrate was changing.

The World Ends Locally

The apocalypse is usually misunderstood.

People imagine it as a single terminal event, one grand curtain drop, one cinematic detonation. But worlds end constantly. Quietly. Locally. Unevenly.

The world of handwritten letters ended.

The world of door-to-door salesmen ended.

The world of answering machines ended.

The world of record stores ended.

The world of smoking sections, paper maps, classified ads, three television channels, memorized phone numbers, and childhoods without surveillance ended.

The people remained.

The planet remained.

But a world ended.

This is how history actually works. Not as one apocalypse, but as billions of little apocalypses braided together. A mode of life collapses, and the survivors call it growing up. A technology disappears, and the culture calls it progress. A social grammar evaporates, and only the old notice that the air tastes different.

Then the old die, and the vanished world loses its witnesses.

Eventually nobody remembers that reality ever had another interface.

The Present Is Already Ancient

Look carefully at your life.

The apps. The jokes. The anxieties. The brands. The political categories. The dating rituals. The work habits. The entertainment formats. The phrases people use to signal intelligence or virtue or belonging.

Much of it is already aging.

Some of it is already dead and does not know it.

You can feel this most clearly in media. Every form begins as a portal and ends as furniture. Radio was once a ghost in the room. Television was once a national altar. The internet was once a frontier. Social media was once a party, then a battlefield, then a casino, then a psychiatric weather system.

Now artificial intelligence has arrived like a new species of mirror. Not a tool only, but a pressure. A solvent. A strange cognitive heat. It will melt categories we thought were solid: student, writer, programmer, teacher, artist, assistant, expert, amateur, search, memory, originality, work.

People will try to use it to preserve the present.

That is always the first instinct.

Use the new machine to make the old world faster.

But the machine does not merely accelerate the old world. It changes what the world is.

The printing press did not create better monasteries.

Electricity did not create better candles.

The internet did not create better newspapers.

AI will not create better office work.

It will create something else.

And for a while, people will mourn the office work.

Because that is what humans do. We fall in love with the cage after decorating it.

The Fragility of “Worthwhile”

What counts as worthwhile is not fixed.

This may be the most destabilizing fact of all.

A life can be organized around honor, salvation, conquest, scholarship, beauty, family, profit, fame, leisure, health, expression, efficiency, authenticity, influence, or survival. Each era installs its own scoreboard and then pretends the game is natural.

The Roman wanted dignity.

The medieval monk wanted devotion.

The industrialist wanted production.

The influencer wants attention.

The optimizer wants metrics.

The artist wants signal through noise.

None of these are eternal. They are culturally stabilized hallucinations, made durable by repetition, institutions, incentives, and fear.

Then the environment changes.

The old scoreboard remains on the wall, but the game has moved elsewhere.

A person can spend a lifetime climbing a ladder that is being quietly removed from the building.

This is not a reason for despair. It is a reason for lucidity.

If worth is historically constructed, then you are not required to worship the scoreboard of your moment. You can see it. You can study it. You can use it when useful and refuse it when it becomes ridiculous.

You do not have to believe every god of the age.

Especially the ones printed on dashboards.

Enjoy the Sun While It Is on Your Face

is not an argument for nihilism.

It is the opposite.

The fleetingness of a world does not make it meaningless. It makes it luminous.

A summer is not worthless because autumn comes. A song is not a failure because it ends. A childhood is not fake because nobody can live there twice.

The fact that your world is temporary is not an insult to it. It is the condition that makes it precious.

Enjoy the absurdity of now.

Enjoy the strange rituals that future people will find incomprehensible. Enjoy the devices, the clothes, the slang, the music, the memes, the nervous systems we have built out of glass and satellites. Enjoy the little ceremonies of your vanishing tribe.

Enjoy what is entertaining before entertainment changes shape again.

Enjoy what is popular before popularity migrates into another machine.

Enjoy the work, even if the work is historically weird.

Enjoy the city while it is this city.

Enjoy your friends while they are gathered in this configuration of time, before everyone is scattered by marriage, ambition, migration, exhaustion, ideology, children, money, illness, or the ordinary erosion of days.

Enjoy the world while it still knows how to be this world.

Not because it is perfect. It is not. Not because it deserves preservation in amber. It does not. But because you are inside a rare weather pattern of history, a temporary bloom of arrangements that almost no one before you ever saw and almost no one after you will see again.

They all thought it was forever.

It never was.

The speakeasy closes. The empire cracks. The leather jacket becomes vintage. The revolution becomes a photograph. The software becomes legacy code. The future becomes old footage. The child’s imagined tomorrow becomes an artifact from a timeline that never happened.

And still, here you are.

Standing in the temporary light.

The task is not to freeze it. The task is not to pretend it will last. The task is to feel the warmth while it is actually touching you, to look directly at the strange civilization flickering around you, and to understand that this too is a once-in-a-species event.

Do not sleepwalk through the miracle just because it is unstable.

The sun is on your face now.

That is enough reason to open your eyes.
