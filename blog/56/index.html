Consciousness and the Cost of Believing It’s Different

March 23, 2026 / Also on Substack

Richard Feynman’s warning is often quoted as a kind of intellectual proverb: the easiest person to fool is yourself. It sounds modest, almost polite. But taken seriously, it is a destabilizing claim. It implies that error is not an accident—it is a default condition. Not a glitch, but the baseline.

Now place that beside the Copernican principle: we are not special. Not in space, not in time, not in structure. Every time humans have assumed a privileged position—geocentrism, biological exceptionalism, cultural centrality—we’ve been corrected. Not gently, but decisively.

Taken together, these ideas form a kind of epistemic pressure: if we are both systematically self-deceptive and structurally non-special, then any belief that flatters our uniqueness should be treated as high-risk.

And yet, that is precisely where many people place consciousness.

The Last Refuge of Exception

There is a pattern in scientific history. As domains become understood, they lose their mystique. Lightning becomes electromagnetism. Disease becomes microbiology. Life becomes chemistry.

Each time, something that once felt irreducibly special becomes mechanistic, continuous with the rest of nature.

Consciousness, for many, is the last holdout.

It is often framed as fundamentally different: not just complex, but categorically distinct. Something that cannot, in principle, be reduced to neural activity. Something that must “transcend” the brain.

Notice the structure of this belief. It is not merely a hypothesis—it is a preference. A directional pull toward explanations that preserve human centrality. Consciousness becomes the final axis along which we remain exceptional.

That alone should trigger suspicion.

Motivated Ontology

The problem is not that non-reductive theories of consciousness are impossible. The problem is that the desire for them is highly non-random.

We are not neutral observers sampling hypotheses uniformly. We are systems shaped by evolutionary and cultural incentives—systems that benefit from believing we are more than matter, more than mechanism, more than transient configurations of atoms.

This creates what you might call a motivated ontology: a bias not just in what we believe, but in what kinds of explanations we find emotionally acceptable.

If a theory implies that consciousness is an emergent property of neural computation—finite, fragile, and reproducible—it often feels reductive, even offensive. If a theory implies that consciousness is fundamental, irreducible, or non-local, it feels expansive, meaningful.

But emotional valence is not evidence. It is a distortion field.

Feynman’s point lands here with full force: the beliefs that feel most satisfying are precisely the ones most likely to be self-deceptive.

The Compression Test

One way to think about scientific progress is through compression. Good theories compress reality—they explain more with less. They reduce the number of independent assumptions.

From this perspective, a brain-based account of consciousness is not just philosophically austere; it is computationally efficient. It aligns with everything else we know about biology, physics, and information processing. It does not introduce new ontological categories without necessity.

By contrast, placing consciousness “outside” the brain often requires expanding the ontology—introducing new primitives, new laws, or poorly specified substrates. It increases the descriptive burden.

That doesn’t make it wrong. But it raises the cost. And given the Copernican prior—that we are not special—the higher-cost explanation carries an additional penalty.

You are betting that, in this one domain, the long historical trend breaks.

Local Illusions, Global Patterns

There is also a scale problem. From the inside, consciousness feels absolute. Immediate. Irreducible. It is the one thing we cannot step outside of.

But this is exactly what you would expect from a system that models itself.

A sufficiently complex information-processing system—recursive, predictive, self-referential—will generate a stable internal perspective. It will construct a “center” because that is computationally efficient. It reduces the dimensionality of decision-making. It organizes experience around an agent.

From within that system, the constructed center feels fundamental. But that does not mean it is.

The Copernican move is to step back and ask: is this feeling evidence of a special ontology, or is it evidence of a common computational architecture?

History suggests the latter.

Double Suspicion

So the correct stance is not simple skepticism. It is targeted skepticism—what you might call double suspicion.

First, suspect the intuitive belief: that consciousness must be something more than the brain because it feels that way.

Second, suspect the motivation behind the belief: the subtle, pervasive desire for human exceptionalism.

When both align—when a belief both feels right and elevates our status—it should be treated as maximally suspect.

This is not cynicism. It is calibration.

The Inversion

There is a deeper inversion here. The move to place consciousness inside the brain is often described as reductive, as if it diminishes something essential.

But it may do the opposite.

If consciousness is what complex matter does under certain conditions—if it is an emergent property of organized information—then it is not a rare exception. It is a pattern. A process. Potentially widespread, perhaps even inevitable wherever the right structures arise.

In that view, consciousness is not less significant. It is more general.

We are not special because we have it. We are instances of a broader phenomenon.

Where This Leaves You

The uncomfortable implication is that the most meaningful questions about consciousness may not be metaphysical, but engineering questions. What architectures generate it? What constraints shape it? How does it scale, fragment, or merge?

And that shifts the frame. You are no longer a witness to a mysterious, isolated essence. You are a particular configuration in a larger design space—a system that can, in principle, be understood, replicated, and transformed.

That realization can feel like a loss of privilege. But it is also an expansion of scope.

Because if consciousness is not outside nature, then it is fully inside the domain of what can be explored, modified, and extended.

And that means the story is not about protecting a special status. It is about participating in the unfolding structure of a process we are only beginning to map.
