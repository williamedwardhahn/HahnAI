Progress, Corrupted

March 11, 2026 / Also on Substack

A few hundred years ago, the idea of social progress barely existed. History was understood as cyclical, tragic, or ordained. Empires rose and collapsed. Plagues came and went. The future was not assumed to be better than the past. Improvement was possible, but it was not inevitable.

Then something unusual happened. During the Enlightenment and the early industrial era, a new cultural operating system emerged: the belief that human society could steadily improve. Science advanced. Technology accelerated. Political rights expanded. For the first time, people began to treat the future as an engineering problem.

This idea—progress—became one of the most powerful psychological engines ever invented.

And precisely because it was powerful, it became vulnerable.

The Attack Surface of Goodness

In cybersecurity there is a concept called an attack surface: the places where a system is exposed to manipulation. These are rarely the strongest parts of the system. Attackers do not try to smash the vault door. They look for the unguarded port, the forgotten credential, the trusting user.

Human morality has an attack surface too.

Compassion, fairness, and empathy are not weaknesses in themselves. They are essential features of social intelligence. But they are also open interfaces. They rely on trust. They assume good faith. They are designed to respond to suffering.

That makes them programmable.

If a story can trigger empathy—if it can convincingly frame itself as a struggle for justice or equality—then the story can bypass many of the defenses people normally apply to power. Criticism becomes morally dangerous. Skepticism begins to feel cruel. The narrative itself becomes armored.

The result is an unusual strategic inversion: instead of attacking where a society is strong, influence flows through where it is morally sensitive.

Narrative as a Control System

Modern societies are coordinated less by force than by narrative. Laws, institutions, and norms are downstream of shared stories about what is legitimate and what is unjust.

If you control the narrative around progress, you control the direction of the system.

This does not require a conspiracy in the cinematic sense. Complex systems often drift toward equilibria that reward certain behaviors. Political movements, bureaucracies, media institutions, and corporations all discover—often independently—that moral language is a powerful lever.

Once discovered, the incentive structure locks in.

If every policy can be framed as a moral necessity, opposition becomes ethically suspect. The debate shifts from whether a policy works to whether disagreement signals moral failure. Under those conditions, power expands almost frictionlessly.

Not because people are malicious—but because the system rewards actors who successfully wrap themselves in the language of virtue.

The Paradox of Progress

This creates a paradox.

The ideal of progress originally functioned as a defense against tyranny. It justified expanding rights, limiting arbitrary authority, and empowering individuals.

But when the language of progress becomes monopolized—when it becomes the only morally legitimate direction—then the concept flips.

Now the accusation of being “against progress” becomes a regulatory mechanism. It polices thought. It disciplines dissent. And people internalize it, questioning their own objections before they even articulate them.

The psychological defense system shuts down from the inside.

The individual is not defeated by force. They are convinced that resistance itself would be immoral.

Reclaiming Moral Autonomy

None of this means compassion is a mistake, or that the pursuit of justice should be abandoned. Those instincts remain essential to any healthy civilization.

The real challenge is subtler.

A society must learn to defend its moral instincts without surrendering its capacity for critical thought. It must distinguish between genuine progress and rhetorical progress—between improvements in human freedom and narratives that merely claim that mantle.

In other words, compassion cannot remain an open attack surface.

It has to be paired with intellectual sovereignty.

The Next Evolution of Progress

The next stage of progress may not be technological or economic. It may be cognitive.

Human beings will need a deeper literacy in systems, incentives, and narrative dynamics. We will need to understand how moral language interacts with power the way engineers understand feedback loops in machines.

Because the ideal of progress itself is too important to abandon. It remains one of humanity’s most radical inventions—the belief that we can redesign the future rather than merely inherit it.

But that invention only works if the public can recognize when the language of progress is illuminating the path forward… and when it is quietly being used to steer the entire system somewhere else.
