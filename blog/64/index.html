Hard to imagine

June 11, 2026 / Also on Substack

The Old City Was a Family

The ancient city-state was not simple because ancient people were simple.

It was simple because the model was compressible.

At the center was a king and a queen. Around them were priests, warriors, farmers, builders, mothers, children, elders. The whole city could be understood as a scaled-up household, an extended family with walls, fields, rituals, enemies, ancestors, and gods. A three-year-old could understand the shape of it.

There is the father. There is the mother. There is the house. There is the fire. There is the family. We work so the house survives.

This does not mean the ancient world was morally better. It was brutal, hierarchical, violent, and often arbitrary. But it had one great cognitive advantage over the modern world: people could hold the social order in their heads.

They knew what the city was. They knew who ruled it. They knew what their labor fed. They knew what stories explained it. They knew where they stood in the larger body.

The model was small enough to be lived inside.

The Modern State Cannot Be Held in the Mind

Now ask an ordinary person how their own government works.

What is Congress?

How does it operate?

What do politicians actually do all day?

What is an agency? What is a regulator? What is a committee? What is a continuing resolution? What is administrative law? What is the difference between the legal code, the tax code, the budget, the bureaucracy, the courts, the executive, the lobbyists, the parties, the donors, the consultants, and the permanent machinery that continues regardless of who wins the election?

Most people do not have a model of their government. They have fragments. Symbols. Team colors. Headlines. Vibes.

They know the faces. They do not know the machine.

And this is not a personal failure. It is a structural one.

Consider the tax code by itself. Reading it is already an inhuman task. Not difficult in the ordinary sense, like learning a language or rebuilding an engine. Inhuman in the literal sense: it exceeds the natural scale of individual cognition. It is too large, too recursive, too cross-referenced, too conditional, too full of exceptions to exceptions.

Now scale that up to the whole operating system of the state.

The modern citizen is asked to participate in a system they cannot visualize, cannot audit, cannot narrate, and cannot meaningfully compress.

The result is not merely political confusion. It is existential confusion.

Because a society is not only a machine for distributing resources. It is a machine for distributing meaning.

Meaning Requires a Model

Human beings do not simply need food, shelter, safety, and entertainment.

They need to know what game they are playing.

They need to know what their labor points toward. They need to know how their effort connects to a larger structure. They need to know why their sacrifices matter.

This does not require perfect knowledge. No peasant in an ancient city understood the full grain logistics of the kingdom. No sailor in the British Empire understood every treaty, company charter, naval route, and imperial accounting mechanism.

But they had a usable model.

The empire was a crown, a flag, a family, a mission, a map painted red. The model may have been incomplete. It may have been propagandistic. It may have concealed violence and exploitation. But it was communicable.

That communicability mattered.

The British Empire did not succeed only because it had ships, banks, factories, and guns. It succeeded because it had a story architecture capable of nesting a gigantic bureaucracy inside a simple mental image.

Crown. Country. Civilization. Service.

The person did not need to understand the machinery in order to feel located within it.

That is the strange power of a successful political model. It compresses complexity into a symbol without fully destroying the complexity underneath.

A flag is a zip file for civilization.

The Danger of the Human Face

There is an obvious danger here.

The easiest way to make a system understandable is to give it a face.

The king. The emperor. The president. The founder. The genius. The savior. The father of the nation.

Modern people are right to be suspicious of this. Idolizing individuals is one of the great political errors. It is how adults turn back into children. It is how institutions become cults. It is how a population gives away its agency in exchange for emotional simplicity.

The face is too compressible.

It turns the terrifying complexity of history into one man standing on a balcony.

This is why modern democracies have tried to shift legitimacy away from persons and toward procedures. We do not worship the king. We respect the law. We do not kneel before bloodlines. We submit to constitutions, elections, courts, rights, offices, rules.

This was a genuine moral advance.

But it created a new problem.

Procedures do not automatically generate meaning.

A constitution may be wiser than a king, but it is harder to love. A bureaucracy may be fairer than a household patriarch, but it is harder to imagine. A regulatory agency may prevent disaster, but no child dreams of dying for administrative continuity.

The modern world solved the problem of personal idolatry by building systems so abstract that almost no one can emotionally inhabit them.

We removed the false father and replaced him with a PDF.

The Crisis of Cognitive Legibility

The deepest political crisis of the modern world may not be polarization, corruption, inequality, or institutional decay.

It may be cognitive illegibility.

The systems we live inside have become too complex to form stable mental models of them.

This is true not only of government. It is true of finance, medicine, education, media, supply chains, software, law, science, and war. We are surrounded by systems whose outputs shape our lives but whose internal workings are mostly invisible to us.

The ancient farmer may have feared the king, but he knew where the king lived.

The modern citizen fears “the system,” but cannot point to it.

This produces a very specific kind of madness. When people cannot model power, they mythologize it. They invent demons. They invent secret rooms. They invent omnipotent villains. They invent hidden hands because the visible hands are too many, too diffuse, too procedural, too boring, too technical, too deeply nested in institutional fog.

Conspiracy theory is often bad cognition, but it is also a hunger for compression.

It is the mind trying to turn a data center back into a campfire story.

The Meta-Goal

Every society needs goals.

Build the wall. Harvest the field. Defeat the invader. Serve the crown. Expand the frontier. Win the war. Land on the moon. Cure the disease.

But above ordinary goals there is a higher-order goal: the goal of making the goal understandable.

A civilization must not only coordinate action. It must coordinate imagination.

People need to understand what they are part of. Not perfectly. Not simplistically. But enough that their labor can connect to a shared picture of reality.

This is the neglected function of leadership, architecture, ritual, education, public language, and institutional design. Their job is not merely to inform. Their job is to compress.

Not to lie. Not to manipulate. Not to reduce the world to propaganda.

To compress truthfully.

A good social model is like a good map. It leaves things out, but not the important things. It simplifies without falsifying the terrain. It gives the traveler enough structure to move.

A bad model is not merely wrong. It strands people.

And this is where we are now. Millions of people wake up inside systems they cannot understand, work jobs whose relationship to the whole is unclear, pay taxes into machinery they cannot inspect, vote for representatives whose actual function they barely grasp, and consume narratives designed less to clarify reality than to harvest attention from confusion.

They are not stupid.

They are model-starved.

The New Political Task

The future will not belong simply to the society with the best laws, the most advanced technology, or the largest economy.

It will belong to the society that can make itself mentally inhabitable again.

This does not mean returning to kings and queens. It does not mean dressing bureaucracy in royal costume. It does not mean surrendering to charismatic faces because complexity makes us tired.

It means building institutions that can be understood at multiple levels.

A child should be able to grasp the basic story. A citizen should be able to understand the operating structure. An expert should be able to inspect the details. The model should scale from nursery explanation to constitutional analysis without breaking.

That is the standard.

Not simplicity.

Scalable intelligibility.

The ancient city had simplicity without scale. The modern state has scale without simplicity. The next civilization has to solve for both.

It must be complex enough to run a planetary technological society, but legible enough that human beings can still locate themselves inside it. It must be abstract enough to avoid idol worship, but concrete enough to generate loyalty, sacrifice, and shared purpose. It must be procedural without becoming soulless. Symbolic without becoming childish. Large without becoming invisible.

This is not a cosmetic problem. It is not a messaging problem. It is not about better slogans.

It is a design problem at the deepest level of civilization.

A society is not only made of laws and roads and servers and armies. It is made of the models its people carry in their heads. When those models collapse, the society continues for a while as machinery. Then as habit. Then as theater. Then as ruins.

The work now is to build forms of collective life that people can understand without being deceived.

To make the real visible.

To give the machine a map.

To make civilization imaginable again.
