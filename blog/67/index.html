Kicking rocks

June 26, 2026 / Also on Substack

The Most Expensive Bit in the World

At the end of the match, after the chants and the flags and the crying men painted in national colors, after the drone shots of the stadium and the billion-dollar broadcast apparatus, after the police lines and the private jets and the merchandise and the advertising slots and the tribal hymns of temporary belonging, the planet receives its prize.

One bit of information.

Team A scored more than Team B.

Or it did not.

That is the payload.

Not a theorem. Not a cure. Not a bridge across the abyss. Not a new way to feed a child, clean a river, stabilize a climate, align an intelligence, or repair a civilization losing coherence at the speed of light.

One bit.

Yes or no.

A tiny switch flipped in the skull of the species.

And for this switch, we build temples.

The Stadium Is a Computer

A World Cup match looks like sport, but at sufficient scale it becomes computation.

Twenty-two bodies are placed inside a bounded field. They obey rules. They exchange energy. They generate branching possibilities. The crowd becomes a pressure gradient. The cameras become sensors. The referees become error-correction. The commentators become compression algorithms, turning chaos into narrative. The betting markets assign probabilities. The nations assign meaning.

The whole machine runs for ninety minutes, plus whatever injury time reality demands.

Then the computation halts.

Output: one winner.

In information theory, this is almost comically sparse. The entire planetary apparatus collapses into a binary distinction: advance or go home, glory or humiliation, we are chosen or we are cursed.

A civilization-sized machine produces a coin flip with footnotes.

The question is not whether the game is beautiful. Of course it is beautiful. Humans are very good at making beautiful traps. The question is whether beauty is a sufficient defense for what the trap consumes.

The Cost of Knowing

How much does the bit cost?

Not just the ticket price, not just the broadcasting rights, not just the stadiums and security and hotels and flights and advertising markets. Those are the visible costs, the parts crude enough to enter a spreadsheet.

The deeper cost is attention.

Attention is the root currency of civilization. Before money, before labor, before political will, before technological progress, there is attention. What a society attends to becomes real. What it ignores becomes fate.

A billion minds pointed in the same direction is not entertainment. It is a planetary steering event.

For weeks, the emotional bandwidth of the species is leased to a ball.

There are tears for missed penalties. There is rage over officiating. There is metaphysical despair because a man’s ankle was three inches offside. Entire nations metabolize victory and defeat as if history itself has rendered judgment.

Meanwhile, the machinery of the century keeps moving.

Artificial intelligence accelerates. Synthetic biology matures. Climate systems destabilize. Nuclear risk persists. Financial systems become more abstract and more fragile. Children are born into networks that will shape their minds before they can speak. Governments try to regulate technologies they barely understand. Markets optimize for engagement while human attention becomes strip-mined terrain.

And still, the ball moves left to right.

The Great Filter May Be Boring

People imagine the Great Filter as spectacular.

A flash in the sky. A grey goo horizon. A war. A plague. A machine god waking up angry. Some cinematic terminal event worthy of the species that failed to prevent it.

But perhaps the Great Filter is not dramatic.

Perhaps it is not even hidden in the future.

Perhaps the Great Filter is the inability of intelligent life to allocate attention at civilizational scale.

Not stupidity. Not evil. Not even ignorance. Something stranger and more humiliating: misalignment between what feels meaningful locally and what matters globally.

A creature evolved to care about the tribe, the hunt, the rival, the festival, the contest, the story. Then one day that creature inherits nuclear physics, machine learning, orbital mechanics, gene editing, global supply chains, and planet-scale coordination problems.

But its nervous system still wants the drum circle.

It still wants the flag.

It still wants us versus them.

It still wants to know who won.

The apocalypse does not need to arrive wearing a skull mask. It can arrive wearing a jersey.

Bread, Circus, and Bandwidth

The old phrase is bread and circuses, but the modern version is more precise.

Calories and attention.

Keep the organism fed. Keep the mind occupied.

The ancient circus was local, bounded, intermittent. The modern circus is ambient, global, high-definition, algorithmically amplified, financially optimized, emotionally weaponized. It is not one arena. It is the atmosphere.

Sport is only one province of the larger empire. There is also celebrity scandal, political theater, streaming wars, culture war, infinite feeds, public meltdowns, prestige dramas, toy financial assets, lifestyle aesthetics, moral panics, and outrage packaged for daily consumption.

The genius of the circus is that it does not feel like control. It feels like life.

It feels like friendship, family, memory, childhood, identity, ritual, release. It feels like something sacred precisely because it colonizes the machinery by which humans generate meaning.

That is why critique sounds monstrous. Tell someone the game is a distraction, and they hear you attacking their father, their city, their childhood, their dead grandfather’s favorite team, the one good thing they have left after work has hollowed them out.

This is how the trap protects itself.

It does not merely entertain you.

It becomes you.

The Ball and the Starship

A species has only so much surplus.

Surplus energy. Surplus cognition. Surplus courage. Surplus institutional capacity. Surplus willingness to care about things not immediately visible.

That surplus can be converted into cathedrals, empires, wars, telescopes, vaccines, starships, or spectacles.

The future is built out of whatever a civilization can afford to take seriously.

This is why the ball is not trivial. It is not trivial because nothing that captures planetary attention is trivial. A thing does not have to be fake to be dangerous. It can be genuinely moving, genuinely communal, genuinely skillful, genuinely human—and still be an evolutionary dead end.

A peacock’s tail is beautiful too.

Beauty can bankrupt an organism.

A civilization can drape itself in rituals so emotionally satisfying that it never notices the oxygen leaving the room.

Kicking Rocks

There is an old insult buried inside the phrase: go kick rocks.

It means go waste your time. Go do something pointless. Go exhaust your body against matter that does not care.

But perhaps it is more than an insult. Perhaps it is a fork in the road.

One path leads outward. Toward intelligence, coordination, responsibility, stars. Toward becoming the kind of species that can survive contact with its own tools. Toward learning how to think at the scale of what it has built.

The other path loops back into the field.

Kick the ball. Watch the ball. Argue about the ball. Buy the shirt. Hate the rival. Refresh the feed. Wait for the next tournament. Tell yourself this is culture, this is passion, this is what makes us human.

Maybe it is.

And maybe that is the problem.

The Revaluation

The point is not to abolish games.

A civilization without play would be a machine not worth building. Play is ancient intelligence. Play is how mammals rehearse reality. Play is how children learn rules, bodies learn timing, groups learn trust. A world with no games would be sterile and brittle.

The point is proportion.

The point is to ask what happens when play stops being rehearsal and becomes replacement. When the symbolic contest consumes the energy required for the real one. When the scoreboard becomes more emotionally available than the future.

The danger is not that people enjoy soccer.

The danger is that we have become exquisitely sensitive to fake stakes and almost numb to real ones.

A penalty kick can spike the heart of a nation. An extinction curve barely registers. A referee’s mistake can produce riots. A misaligned incentive structure inside an artificial intelligence lab sounds abstract. A championship can make strangers embrace in the street. The slow liquidation of the biosphere becomes background noise.

This is not a moral failure of individuals. It is a design failure of civilization.

Our attention systems are calibrated for the village and deployed against the planet.

The Bit Is a Mirror

At the end of the game, the bit arrives.

Team A beat Team B.

But underneath that bit is another one, much larger and more terrible.

What kind of species are we?

Are we the animal that can notice the trap while still inside it? Are we capable of loving our games without being ruled by them? Can we build rituals that point us toward survival rather than away from it? Can we make the future emotionally real before it becomes catastrophic?

The World Cup is not merely a sporting event. It is a diagnostic instrument. It reveals the power of synchronized attention. It shows that humanity can still gather around one object, one story, one shared field of meaning.

That power is not the problem.

The problem is that we keep spending it on almost nothing.

Imagine turning that same civilizational lens toward the hard problems. Imagine making alignment, energy, water, education, planetary repair, and spacefaring destiny feel as immediate as extra time. Imagine children growing up with heroes who stabilize systems, decode biology, build habitats, defend attention, and teach machines to serve life. Imagine a world where the great contests are not abandoned, but upgraded.

Not less passion.

Better targets.

The Larger Game

The ball is round because it can roll forever.

That is the seduction. Another match, another season, another cup, another bit. The loop can run indefinitely while the world outside the stadium changes phase.

But the species does not have indefinitely.

We are crossing thresholds now. Not mythic thresholds. Technical ones. Computational ones. Thermodynamic ones. Coordination thresholds. Intelligence thresholds. The old games are still running, but the substrate has changed beneath them.

We are no longer villagers with flags.

We are a planetary nervous system deciding whether to wake up.

The invitation is not to sneer at the crowd. The crowd is us. The hunger for belonging is real. The need for beauty is real. The longing to dissolve into something larger than the self is real.

But there are larger things available now than nations chasing a ball.

There is a planet to stabilize. There are minds to build carefully. There are oceans to understand. There are children to prepare for a world no ancestor could have imagined. There are stars overhead, not as decoration, but as a standing challenge.

We can kick rocks.

Or we can learn to leave them.
