Part I · Why Deep Learning

A Short History of Thinking Machines

We shall find it impossible to refrain from asking ourselves what the end of this mighty movement is to be.

Samuel Butler, 1863

It is hard to appreciate how new software is — even the idea of it. When Edsger Dijkstra, later a Turing Award winner, applied for a marriage license in 1957, the authorities asked for his occupation. He wrote “Programmer.” It was rejected: there was no such job in the Netherlands at the time.

Computation as a natural science

Donald Knuth has said: “Science is what we understand well enough to explain to a computer. Art is everything else we do.”1 That cannot be said without controversy, so I will add some of my own. In this decade, if you are not using a computer, you are probably not doing science; it is hard to imagine a modern study that does not use one for collecting, analyzing, visualizing or communicating its data. This book takes the view that computers are more than tools. They are the star of the show: a new framework within which humanity can build its next generation of understanding.

1 Donald E. Knuth, foreword to Marko Petkovšek, Herbert S. Wilf and Doron Zeilberger, A = B (Wellesley, MA: A K Peters, 1996), vii.

In his original work Alan Turing presented a new mathematical object, as fundamental as the vector or the derivative: the computer program. It took decades for mathematicians and scientists to agree that programs are mathematical objects at all. Peter Denning goes further: “Computing is now a natural science,” he wrote in 2007; it “is no longer a science of just the artificial. It is the study of information processes, natural and artificial.”2 Physicists find information processes in quantum waves, biologists in DNA, cognitive scientists in patterns of brain activity.

2 Peter J. Denning, “Computing Is a Natural Science,” Communications of the ACM 50, no. 7 (2007): 13–18.

The early giants — Babbage, Turing, von Neumann — had no training in computer science; the subject did not exist. They were mathematicians and natural scientists. In my opinion, one of the unfortunate turns in academic history was the creation of separate computer science departments, which treated computing as its own discipline, cut off from natural science, mathematics, philosophy and language. I suspect it came down partly to cost: I can imagine department heads forced to choose between lab rats and workstations, and when I look at academic labs today I don’t see many that have both. Neuroscience, the field that most needs to think about computation, is the one that has most often ignored it. Richard Feynman is reported to have said, in a lecture at Bell Laboratories, “I don’t believe in computer science.”3 I believe in it — as a natural science, a branch of physics and mathematics. And as the rest of this chapter shows, the pioneers were thinking about brains all along.

3 Recorded talk, usually dated to 1985 and captioned as given at Bell Laboratories. He went on: “To me, science is the study of the behavior of nature. And engineering or applied things is the behavior of things we make.”

Let us compute

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz dreamed of settling disputes by calculation. Instead of dueling, as Gregory Chaitin retells it, two people should be able to “just sit down and say, ‘Gentlemen, let us compute!’ ”4

4 Gregory Chaitin, “The Perfect Language,” Inference: International Review of Science 1, no. 3 (2015). Leibniz’s own word was the Latin calculemus, “let us calculate.”

Charles Babbage, Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge from 1828 to 1839, was tasked with eliminating errors in mathematical tables, and is said to have exclaimed, “I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam!”5 He planned a machine called the Analytical Engine. It was never completed, yet it drove the world’s imagination. One admirer wrote that Babbage “had taught wheelwork to think.”6 According to the historian Doron Swade, Babbage boasted that his machines “would produce the correct result or would jam but that they would never deceive.”7

5 The episode dates from 1821, when Babbage and John Herschel were checking astronomical tables; the words are from Babbage’s account in H. W. Buxton, Memoir of the Life and Labours of the Late Charles Babbage Esq. F.R.S., ed. Anthony Hyman (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1988), 46, as cited by Doron Swade, “Automatic Computation: Charles Babbage and Computational Method,” The Rutherford Journal 3 (2010).
6 Buxton, Memoir, written 1872–1880. The full sentence: “the marvelous pulp and fibre of a brain had been substituted by brass and iron, he had taught wheelwork to think.”
7 Doron D. Swade, “Redeeming Charles Babbage’s Mechanical Computer,” Scientific American, February 1993.

Ada Lovelace saw further than anyone, at least a century ahead of her time. In 1844 she wrote that “it does not appear to me that cerebral matter need be more unmanageable to mathematicians than sidereal & planetary matter & movements,” and that she hoped “to bequeath to the generations a calculus of the nervous system.”8 A calculus of the nervous system is not a bad description of what you will build in this book.

8 Ada Lovelace, letter to Woronzow Greig, 15 November 1844, in Betty Alexandra Toole, Ada, the Enchantress of Numbers (Mill Valley, CA: Strawberry Press, 1992). The sentence on cerebral matter is from her correspondence of the same period.

The mechanical kingdom

In 1863 Samuel Butler connected Darwin’s new theory of evolution to the machines spreading everywhere around him.9 He looked at the “primordial types of mechanical life” — the lever, the wedge, the inclined plane, the screw and the pulley — and then at the machinery of the steamship Great Eastern, and found himself “almost awestruck” at how fast the mechanical world advanced compared with the slow progress of animals and plants. “In what direction is it tending? What will be its upshot?”

9 Samuel Butler, “Darwin among the Machines,” The Press (Christchurch, New Zealand), 13 June 1863.

Butler’s image was a new branch on the tree of life: as the vegetable kingdom developed from the mineral, and the animal from the vegetable, “so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up.” He described a pocket watch the way a naturalist describes a turtle, as “the beautiful structure of the little animal,” descended from the cumbrous clocks of the thirteenth century, and predicted that clocks might one day go extinct like the earlier saurians. It is a metaphor, not biology, but a useful one: if the machines really are a new branch on the tree of life, we should expect the world to adapt to them on the scale of earlier upheavals, like the rise of the animals.

Turing’s surprise

By 1950 the idea of a digital computer was, in Turing’s words, already “an old one.”10 His most detailed knowledge of the Analytical Engine came from Lady Lovelace’s 1843 memoir, which states: “The Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.” Hartree had added in 1949 that this did not rule out electronic equipment that would “think for itself.”

10 Alan Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Mind 59, no. 236 (1950): 433–460.

Turing took the objection that a machine can never surprise us and turned it around. “Machines take me by surprise with great frequency,” he wrote, largely because he did not do enough calculation to know what to expect. The belief that machines cannot surprise us, he argued, rests on the false assumption that “as soon as a fact is presented to a mind all consequences of that fact spring into the mind simultaneously with it.” His goal was to “investigate the question as to whether it is possible for machinery to show intelligent behaviour,”11 and with Turing, it is my contention “that machines can be constructed which will simulate the behaviour of the human mind very closely.”12

11 Alan Turing, “Intelligent Machinery,” 1948.
12 Alan Turing, “Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory,” c. 1951.

John McCarthy, who coined the term artificial intelligence, saw two roads: imitate the nervous system or human psychology, insofar as we understand them, or study the problems the world presents to anything trying to achieve goals. “I would say that the computer-science approach is the one that so far has had the most success.”13 Machine learning is nearly as old: Arthur Samuel’s 1959 “Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers” reported a program that learned “to play a better game of checkers than can be played by the person who wrote the program.” Konrad Zuse, who independently pioneered much of early hardware and software, later recalled that he had “realized that there is no border-line” between calculating and thinking.14

13 John McCarthy, “Artificial Intelligence,” interview by Jeffrey Mishlove, Thinking Allowed (undated; the series ran 1986–2002).
14 Konrad Zuse, “Some Remarks on the History of Computing in Germany,” lecture, Los Alamos, 1976; in N. Metropolis, J. Howlett and G.-C. Rota, eds., A History of Computing in the Twentieth Century (New York: Academic Press, 1980).

From perceptrons to transformers

The neural network road had its own history, and it was not a straight one. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron, a machine that learned to classify patterns by adjusting the strengths of its connections. It was greeted with great excitement. A year later Oliver Selfridge described Pandemonium, a model of perception as a crowd of “demons”: simple ones shouting when they see a feature, such as a line or a corner, and higher ones listening to them and shouting in turn, until a decision demon picks the loudest. Layers of feature detectors, each built on the one below, are exactly what a deep network learns. In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a book that analyzed precisely what a single layer of such units can and cannot compute. Its limits were real, and the book became a major reason that funding and interest drained away from neural network research for more than a decade — one of the “AI winters.”

The spring came in the 1980s. John Hopfield showed how ideas from physics could describe networks of neurons, and in 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams popularized backpropagation, a way of training networks with many layers by sending the error backward through them. Meanwhile, in 1980, Kunihiko Fukushima had built the Neocognitron, a layered network for recognizing visual patterns inspired by the visual cortex. Yann LeCun combined that layered, convolutional design with backpropagation and, from the late 1980s through 1998, developed the convolutional networks known as LeNet, which read handwritten digits well enough to be put to practical use. The MNIST dataset of handwritten digits that came out of that work is the one you will use in Part II.

What these networks lacked was data and speed. Both arrived. ImageNet gathered millions of labeled photographs in some 22,000 categories, a thousand of which were used in an annual competition. In 2012 AlexNet — essentially LeNet’s design made much larger, trained on graphics processors — won that competition by a wide margin, and the field turned on a dime. In 2017 the transformer was built around a mechanism called attention, and transformers became the engine of the language models that reached the public in late 2022.

Rosenblatt’s perceptron, backpropagation, LeCun’s digit reader, AlexNet, attention: you will build a version of every one of them in this book. The history is the syllabus.

Computers for thinking with

There is a second history running alongside the first, about what computers are for. In 1960 J. C. R. Licklider described “man-computer symbiosis”: people and machines working together, each doing what it does best. Douglas Engelbart set out in 1962 to build tools for “augmenting human intellect,” and in a famous 1968 demonstration showed the mouse, hypertext, shared screens and video conferencing, decades before most people had any of them. Seymour Papert, the same Papert who helped write Perceptrons, spent much of his life on children: with colleagues he created the Logo programming language, in which children teach a “turtle” to draw, and in Mindstorms (1980) he argued that children learn powerful ideas best by building things with them. Alan Kay, building on Papert, imagined a personal computer for children of all ages, the Dynabook, a medium for thinking rather than a calculator.

This book belongs to that second history as much as to the first. Its method is Papert’s: you learn how a learning machine works by building one, breaking it and building it again. And the machines themselves are tools for thinking. A network that has learned to see is not only a product to use; it is an experiment you can open up, a theory of vision you can test.
