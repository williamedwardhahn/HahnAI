Part I · Why Deep Learning

A Jungle of Brain Cells

When we talk mathematics, we may be discussing a secondary language, built on the primary language truly used by the central nervous system.

John von Neumann, The Computer and the Brain, 1958

It is difficult to describe the brain, given that I am a brain and cannot be objective in the matter. In this chapter I will focus on one part of the story, the information processing: how a brain learns to adapt itself.

We should be humble about our metaphors. As John Daugman observes, “invariably the explanatory metaphors of a given era incorporate the devices and the spectacles of the day.” The water technology of antiquity underlies the Greek idea of the soul as pneuma and Galen’s four humours; Enlightenment clockwork ticks inside La Mettrie’s L’Homme machine; Victorian steam and hydraulics churn beneath Freud’s unconscious; the telegraph gave Helmholtz his metaphors.1 So yes, “the brain is a computer” is our era’s metaphor. The difference, I will argue, is that this metaphor can be run, tested and built.

1 John G. Daugman, “Brain Metaphor and Brain Theory,” in William Bechtel et al., eds., Philosophy and the Neurosciences: A Reader (Oxford: Blackwell, 2001), 23–24.

Francis Crick called it “the Astonishing Hypothesis”: that “you,” your joys and sorrows, memories and ambitions, “are in fact no more than the behavior of a vast assembly of nerve cells and their associated molecules.”2 Neural network models rest on that hypothesis, and on an older discovery: Ramón y Cajal’s “neuron doctrine,” recognized with the 1906 Nobel Prize he shared with Golgi, which established that the brain is built from separate cells. If the brain is made of units, it can be modeled as a network of units.

2 Francis Crick, The Astonishing Hypothesis: The Scientific Search for the Soul (New York: Scribner, 1994).

Three generations of neural networks

In 1943 Warren McCulloch, a neuroscientist, and Walter Pitts, a logician, developed the first generation of neural networks as simplified models of nervous function. Real neurons are so complex that fully simulating the chemistry of even one cell is beyond the fastest computers, so a neural network models only a neuron’s information processing. The McCulloch–Pitts neuron was a switch, either all on or all off. Networks of such switches could reproduce simple logic, and perhaps, it seemed, the brain was a system for implementing logic in switches, like the early telephone exchanges.

The second generation gives each neuron a continuous, graded output, and almost all of the networks in use today, including every network in this book, are of this kind. Its key development was backpropagation, which adjusts a network’s weights to shrink a single number measuring how wrong its answers are. You will build exactly this, by hand, in Part III.

The third generation, as Wolfgang Maass defined it in 1997, uses spiking neurons, which communicate with brief pulses timed in continuous time, much as real neurons do. Running alongside all three generations is a thread from physics: John Hopfield’s networks of the 1980s borrowed the mathematics physicists use for magnetic materials and described a network’s computation as rolling downhill on an energy landscape. That view of neural computation as a physical process suggests networks might one day be built directly in hardware rather than simulated on digital computers.

Deep learning’s big idea is learning features from data instead of designing them by hand, layer upon layer. Despite decades of research a broad theory of the brain remains elusive, and when Demis Hassabis, co-founder of DeepMind, said in 2010 that “if you last looked seriously at neuroscience circa 2005 — you are out of date,” he might have been talking to most of us.3

3 Demis Hassabis, “A Systems Neuroscience Approach to Building AGI,” Singularity Summit, San Francisco, August 2010.

Randomly arranged neuron-like elements

Turing anticipated much of this. As Jack Copeland and Diane Proudfoot put it, “few realize that Alan Turing wrote a blueprint for much of the connectionist project as early as 1948.”4 Turing suggested that training could make some pathways through such a network effective and others ineffective, and, so far as is known, he “was the first person to consider building computing machines out of trainable networks of randomly arranged neuron-like elements.”

4 B. Jack Copeland and Diane Proudfoot, “Turing’s Neural Networks of 1948,” AlanTuring.net (2000); see also Copeland’s introduction to “Intelligent Machinery” in Copeland, ed., The Essential Turing (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2004).

Randomly arranged. Keep that word in mind. A child born in France learns French, and a child born in Spain learns Spanish. Our DNA cannot contain instructions for a machine that understands French or Spanish; there is too much information for that. Instead it encodes a semi-general-purpose learning machine whose particular connection strengths are not specified at birth. They start from something close to a random initial condition, and the brain develops by strengthening the connections that solve problems.

There is evidence that the same learning machinery serves many purposes. The Common Cortical Algorithm hypothesis holds that much of perception may come from a single learning algorithm. Ferrets whose visual inputs have been rewired to their auditory cortex can learn to see, and in hamsters rewired to their touch cortex, the touch neurons begin to respond like visual ones.5

5 Laurie von Melchner, Sarah L. Pallas and Mriganka Sur, “Visual Behaviour Mediated by Retinal Projections Directed to the Auditory Pathway,” Nature 404 (2000): 871–876; Christine Métin and Douglas O. Frost, “Visual Responses of Neurons in Somatosensory Cortex of Hamsters with Experimentally Induced Retinal Projections to Somatosensory Thalamus,” PNAS 86 (1989): 357–361.

Neurons as animals

The living jungle of brain cells we all carry can be thought of as a microcosm of evolution on a much faster time scale. Each brain cell is like a small animal. It has no idea it is a brain cell, but it lives in an ecosystem of other cells that must cooperate and compete for nutrients and information; without input, neurons atrophy. In the forest, animals compete for places to live and food to eat. In the brain, neurons compete to represent ideas and memories. In the first word of the previous sentence you saw the capital I as a vertical bar, and you have neurons that detect that feature. From it and its neighbors you concluded the word was “In,” and used that to understand the rest of the sentence. The cells that represent that vertical bar had to compete for the job. In ecology we would say they found a niche, the way a squirrel in a mature forest has found its niche among the acorns. Neither the squirrel nor the oak decided to enter into their arrangement; the forest evolved into it. In the same way, the brain starts as a collection of animals that must evolve, over seconds, days and years, into a mature ecosystem.

Culture as software

Perception is built as a hierarchy: the eye and ear pass signals up through stage after stage of processing, each extracting something more abstract than the last, from edges to stripes to a tiger. We pay attention only at the top of this hierarchy. You cannot attend to the stripes on the tiger; you don’t even want to think about the tiger very much. You need to think about running away. If we could hear the language of our eardrums it would make no sense to us; if we could see what falls on our retinas, we could not make sense of it. Our conscious experience lives at the top of a deep-learning-style hierarchy, among symbols, concepts and emotions.

I once asked some students to describe the taste of strawberries in English. After a second, one blurted out “heaven.” There is no good way to put experience into words. How, then, can we do what Turing suggested and teach a computer to “enjoy strawberries and cream”?6

6 Alan Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Mind 59, no. 236 (1950): 433–460.

Much of what we can do comes from culture — software, so to speak — riding on top of our perceptual system, and it is nearly impossible to separate what our brains do from what our culture does. No human is raised in isolation; we need a steady stream of meaningful information to train our neural networks. If that is true of us, it will be true of the machines we build.
