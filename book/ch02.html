Part I · Why Deep Learning

The Pascaline Wager

I do not fear computers. I fear the lack of them.

Isaac Asimov, as quoted in Time, 1978

Blaise Pascal, the seventeenth-century French philosopher, mathematician and physicist, is remembered for two inventions that I think belong together.

The first is his famous wager. Pascal’s Wager says that every one of us bets with our life that God either exists or does not. You cannot sit the bet out; you can only decide how to live given the stakes. Pascal was honest about how uncomfortable that is: “seeing too much to deny Him, and too little to assure me, I am in a pitiful state.”1

1 Blaise Pascal, Pensées, Project Gutenberg edition, http://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-0.txt.

The second is the Pascaline, a mechanical calculator. Pascal built it at nineteen, spurred by the arithmetical labor of his father’s work as supervisor of taxes in Rouen. It added and subtracted directly, and multiplied and divided by repeated addition and subtraction. It was, in a sense, premature: the mechanical arts of his time could not make it cheaply, accurately and strongly enough for long use, and that was not overcome until well into the nineteenth century.2

2 S. Chapman, “Blaise Pascal (1623–1662): Tercentenary of the calculating machine,” Nature 150 (October 31, 1942): 508–509.

So here is what I call the Pascaline Wager. Either artificial intelligence is going to significantly change your life, or it is not; and either you will help shape it, or you will leave that to others. As with Pascal’s original, you cannot decline to bet; not deciding is a decision. And like the Pascaline itself, the machines in question spent a long time looking premature — right up until they weren’t.

AI has arrived

Driving a car, reading a CT scan, writing an essay, generating a photograph of something that never existed: AI has “arrived” in a way previous generations only dreamed of, and virtually all of it is due to one algorithmic idea, artificial neural networks, loosely modeled on the brain. Even the people who built the modern world were surprised. In January 2017, at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Sergey Brin, co-founder of Google, recalled that, having trained as a computer scientist in the 1990s, “everybody knew that AI didn’t work.” “The revolution in deep nets has been very profound, it definitely surprised me, even though I was sitting right there.”3

3 Sergey Brin, “An Insight, An Idea with Sergey Brin,” World Economic Forum Annual Meeting, Davos, January 2017; as reported by the World Economic Forum, “Google co-founder Sergey Brin: I didn’t see AI coming” (2017).

Cats and dogs, before and after 2012

To see what changed, look at a problem computers simply could not solve. Around 1990 Marvin Minsky said it plainly: “Right now there’s no machine that can look around and tell a dog from a cat.”4 As late as 2007, Microsoft researchers built a security test, a CAPTCHA, on the assumption that a computer could not reliably sort photographs of cats from photographs of dogs.5 It was not for lack of trying. Some of the smartest people in the world, Minsky among them, threw every mathematical and computational approach they had at visual recognition, and got slow, incremental progress that never worked well enough to use in the real world — on a job a sharp two-year-old does without much trouble.

4 Marvin Minsky, “Mind as Society,” interview by Jeffrey Mishlove, Thinking Allowed (undated; the series ran 1986–2002).
5 Jeremy Elson, John R. Douceur, Jon Howell and Jared Saul, “Asirra: A CAPTCHA that Exploits Interest-Aligned Manual Image Categorization,” Proceedings of the 14th ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (2007).

Then, in 2012, a deep neural network called AlexNet won the ImageNet competition, sorting photographs into a thousand categories far better than any rival method, and deep learning took over computer vision, then speech, translation and, with the transformer, language. The next chapter tells that story.

Ask yourself what it means for the planet when a problem moves from the impossible category to the not that difficult category. That is what happened to cats and dogs, and it has kept happening, problem after problem.

Placing the bet

People have always been bad at this wager. In 1876, by Thomas Watson’s later account, Bell’s backers offered the telephone patent to Western Union for $100,000, and its president, William Orton, turned it down. A famous committee memo dismissing the telephone as a toy is, alas, a twentieth-century invention.6

6 The memo first appeared in print in 1968; see David Malki, “True Stuff: Alexander Graham Bell vs. Western Union,” Wondermark.

If you do not believe AI is going to be a major force in the world, ask yourself what about nature and science is going to change to stop it. Part of the purpose of this book is to tell you about the emergency we face, and the emergency is not that people are working on AI. It is that not enough people are working on AI. We need students, researchers and practitioners in every field to seriously consider what their field will look like when much of what they do now can be automated.

That’s the wager. I’ve placed my bet. The rest of this book is my attempt to help you place yours with your eyes open, and, better still, to give you the means to invent what comes next rather than wait for it.
