Part I · Why Deep Learning

Eight Million Numbers, One Bit

A man provided with paper, pencil, and rubber, and subject to strict discipline, is in effect a universal machine.

Alan Turing, 1948

In my lab there is a small self-driving rover with a camera. Its controls are reduced to three actions: turn left, turn right, go forward. We taught it to drive a scale road course without writing a single rule about roads. First, people drove it around the course, choosing an action for each camera frame. That gave us a large table of pictures, each labeled left, right or forward. Then a neural network studied the table until, shown a new frame, it chose the action a human would have chosen. Run that network many times a second, and the rover drives the course on its own.

That little machine is this whole book in miniature. Pixels go in, a decision comes out, and in between is a function nobody wrote by hand: it was learned from examples. Everything that follows — the arrays, the guessing, the gradients, the layers, the convolutions — is about how to build that function, and how to understand what it has learned. You will be able to build the rover’s brain yourself by the end of Part IV: it is the recipe of Chapter 16, with three folders of camera frames.

Let’s look more closely at what the problem really is: taking images from a camera and turning them into decisions.

Modern cameras record an enormous number of samples. An 8-megapixel photo has eight million pixels, and a color photo stores three numbers for each one — red, green and blue — so that’s about 24 million numbers written to memory every time you press the button. The cameras in 2026 phones range from 12 to 200 megapixels. Now suppose we want to make one decision from that photograph: are the subject’s eyes closed? If they are, the camera can wait before taking the shot. Eyes open or closed is a single binary digit, 0 for closed, 1 for open. So the camera software has to look at tens of millions of numbers and reduce them to one bit.

That is the real question. How do we recombine millions of numbers into a single bit that tells us whether the people in the scene have their eyes open? This is what deep learning makes possible.

Skills we cannot write down

For most vision tasks it is simply not practical for humans to write out the rules, whether for driving a car or for recognizing handwriting. These are skills we embody, but we have no explicit access to them in a form we could write down as a program.

We do not teach babies to walk by explaining the dynamics of their ankle muscles, and if you think about your toes too much you’re liable to fall down the stairs. Language is the same. We speak and listen in ideas, and it is hard to hear the sounds that make up our words. Repeat a word over and over until you can finally hear the sound you are making. By then it no longer makes sense as an idea; instead of dolphin, it is a strange and surprisingly complex set of noises.

There is a famous saying in AI that the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard. Building machines that see the way a two-year-old does required a different approach to computer science.

You already know the vocabulary

The good news is that you already understand many of the ideas you need. Thirty years ago this would not have been the case, but today everyone reading this knows about digital cameras, pixels, memory and file sizes. The vocabulary of consumer electronics is a Rosetta Stone for deep learning. Even a small child knows how to filter, crop and resize a photo, and that is how we will approach deep learning: as a series of filter and resize operations. A filter can pick out the edges in an image; a resize step, which we will call pooling, shrinks it while keeping what matters.

The rover from the start of this chapter makes the vocabulary concrete. Its table of labeled frames turns a network into a policy: a rule that chooses an action given an image. This is supervised learning; the human drivers provide the supervision in the form of labeled frames.

Photo tagging on social networks worked the same way. Before a system could suggest that you were in a picture, it had to have seen photographs labeled as having you in them, and those labels let it recognize you in new photos, even with a new haircut. Deciding which way to turn the steering wheel from a dash-cam image is, in principle, no different from finding a tumor in a brain scan, or a pattern in a medical, financial or legal document.

A line is a decision

The familiar y=mx+by = mx + b from high school algebra takes center stage in a modern deep learning system. Use the formula as a recipe to build a table of values, graph it, and you get a line. Now think of that line as a decision boundary. On one side we put all the yeses, on the other all the nos. Yes might mean “there’s a good chance this photograph shows skin cancer,” and no, “nothing to worry about.”

Real data can rarely be separated by a single line. The power of deep learning is to combine thousands of these equations into a very complex decision space. No human could tune all those lines. Instead we start with random lines and let the data adjust them as the network learns. The adjusting needs exactly one idea beyond addition and multiplication: the slope, which tells us which way to nudge each number to make the answers less wrong. We will build that from scratch.

How do children learn shapes and colors, or the alphabet? Do you remember learning cursive? We call such tasks child’s play, but they are at the heart of what a brain does. Handwriting recognition turned out to be far harder than anyone expected, until people tried to build a machine to reliably read unreliably written handwriting. The MNIST dataset, born of LeCun’s work on reading digits, is arguably the most studied dataset in neural network research: many thousands of handwritten numerals, 0 through 9. The task is to name each one, as on Sesame Street.

It is hard to believe this problem is hard. We learned it so long ago that we forget, and so much of our brain is devoted to this sort of task that we take for granted doing it in noisy, unfamiliar conditions.

Look at how a handwritten digit becomes data. The original signal was analog, ink on paper, probably from a ballpoint pen. It was digitized by laying a grid over it, like graph paper, and measuring the amount of ink in each square. Now it is a digital signal a program can use. But if you look at two examples of the same digit as two long lists of numbers, which is closer to how the computer sees them, it is very hard to tell that they are the same digit at all.

Our goal is to reduce all of those squares to a single number, the answer to an unambiguous question like “is this a two?” — 0 if not, 1 if so. What recipes could do that? We could add up all the numbers, or multiply them, or average them, or pick one at random. You can imagine many other ideas. These are hand-tuned features: preprocessing steps invented by humans to make the problem easier for the computer. For decades, much of that invention was a compromise with memory. Turing’s theoretical machine had an infinite paper tape; real research groups do not, so many clever techniques were developed to shrink problems down to a size a computer could handle.

A few assumptions, and randomness

Kunihiko Fukushima’s Neocognitron and Yann LeCun’s convolutional networks took a different path. A convolutional network does not make no assumptions about images. It builds in a few strong ones: that nearby pixels matter to each other, and that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears in the picture. Everything else — which edges, which textures, which shapes — it learns from the data, starting from random weights. Hand-tuned features were replaced by a few good assumptions plus randomness.

Randomness has power in its possibility. For something to be random means it is nothing in particular, which, strangely enough, means it can become precisely what you need. Like Turing’s unorganized machines, and like a newborn brain, a neural network starts out randomly connected.

Put another way, you are searching for a function, a mathematical machine, that takes in some input and produces an output that answers your question — a business question, a scientific one, a medical one. It is a strange notion, a mathematical object answering a question, but that is what AI is really about. In a way it was never about computers. It is about finding a mathematical object that we can then instantiate in the physical world with what we call a computer.

Computers were people

Not long ago, computers were people. Computing was an occupation: just as bakers bake, computers computed, following rules with pencil and eraser, one step at a time — precisely the model Turing used to build his formal theory of computation. A human computer given a formula could apply it all day. Write “add” as a function, add(1,1)=1+1\mathrm{add}(1, 1) = 1 + 1, and give it a single letter, ff, as mathematicians like to, and the job is to evaluate ff.

Deep learning turns that job inside out. We know the question we want to ask, and we have examples of answers. What we don’t know is the formula connecting them. So we set up a random neural network that, in some sense, could compute almost any function, and let the examples adjust it until it computes the one we need. Once we have the formula, a computer evaluates it in a flash. The power of deep learning is having a computer find the formula, given only examples of the task.

That idea — find the formula from examples — is the engine of everything in this book. The next chapters ask why it matters to you, where it came from, and what it has to do with brains. Then, in Part II, we start building.
